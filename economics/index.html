3DVR economics notebook

Economics from first principles.

Before arguing about capitalism, socialism, markets, states, or tokens, start lower: how do people coordinate limited time, energy, materials, information, and trust?

What would an economy look like if we intentionally made as many essentials abundant as possible, while using markets mainly to coordinate what remains genuinely scarce?

What does an economy actually need to do?

Decide what gets producedTurn needs, wants, and possibilities into real work.
Coordinate who does the workMatch skills, time, tools, and responsibility.
Move resourcesGet materials, energy, capital, and knowledge where they are useful.
Communicate scarcityMake constraints and changing demand visible enough to act on.
Reward contributionGive people reasons and capacity to keep creating useful value.
Handle risk and failureExperiment without making every mistake catastrophic.
Limit extractionMake it harder to capture value without contributing enough back.
Preserve surplusKeep enough capacity to maintain systems and invest in the future.

Money is one coordination technology

Money can store value, settle exchange, and provide a common unit for comparing very different things. Prices can carry information about scarcity and demand. Firms, markets, governments, cooperatives, families, commons, and open-source communities are all different coordination structures.

No single structure has to solve every problem. A useful economic system can mix them according to what is actually scarce, what needs trust, what benefits from competition, and what works better as shared infrastructure.

Digital abundance changes the problem

Software, knowledge, designs, music, and AI workflows can often be copied at extremely low marginal cost. Once created, restricting every additional copy may be less useful than spreading it.

That suggests a different question: instead of manufacturing scarcity around abundant information, can we make the shared layer open and earn money around things that still carry real marginal cost?

Human timeAttention, care, judgment, teaching, craftsmanship, accountability.
Compute and energyServers, chips, electricity, cooling, bandwidth, physical infrastructure.
Land and materialsHousing, food, factories, minerals, tools, transportation.
Trust and reputationKnowing who will show up, keep promises, and take responsibility.

Open source is an economic structure

Open source creates shared productive capital that many people can use without asking permission or paying for every copy. People and companies can still earn money from implementation, customization, hosting, support, hardware, training, integration, and accountable service.

The commons becomes richer while scarce work remains valuable.

AI makes ownership harder to ignore

If AI lets one person produce what once required a larger team, total productive capacity can rise dramatically. The open question is who owns that productivity gain and how broadly its benefits spread.

Possible answers include concentrated ownership, worker ownership, public infrastructure, cooperatives, open models, local businesses, shared digital commons, or combinations of them. The design matters because the same technology can increase both abundance and concentration.

A 3DVR working hypothesis

Make the abundant layer as open, portable, and reproducible as practical. Charge primarily for what remains scarce: human effort, convenience, compute, physical goods, specialized integration, reliable operation, and accountable service.

Then use the surplus to expand the commons: better tools, better documentation, more local capability, more automation, and lower barriers for the next person.

Questions worth keeping open

  • Essentials: Which goods should society intentionally push toward abundance?
  • Automation: When AI eliminates labor, who should receive the productivity dividend?
  • Markets: Where are prices excellent signals, and where do they miss important human needs?
  • Ownership: Which infrastructure should be private, cooperative, public, local, or part of a commons?
  • Contribution: How do we reward useful work without turning every human interaction into a transaction?
  • Resilience: How do local communities keep enough productive capacity to remain free and adaptable?
