# After Move 37: What Chess and Go Predict About Generative AI

**Thomas M. Stephens · 3DVR Research · September 15, 2026**

> **Working prediction:** Generative AI will follow the broad arc that chess and Go already demonstrated: imitation becomes augmentation, augmentation becomes superhuman performance, and superhuman performance begins producing strategies that humans would not have found on their own. The difference is that generative AI is not playing one game. It is moving into the substrate of software, media, science, business, communication, and eventually physical systems.

## Executive summary

Chess and Go provide a compact preview of what happens when machines become stronger than humans inside an intellectual domain.

The games did not disappear. Humans did not stop playing. Instead, the role of the human changed. Machines became analysis partners, then dominant tactical calculators, then sources of genuinely novel strategic ideas. AlphaGo's famous Move 37 against Lee Sedol became a symbol of that transition because expert observers initially regarded the move as strange, yet it helped overturn centuries of received wisdom about good Go play.

Generative AI appears to be moving through a similar sequence, but at civilizational scale.

The central transition is:

**human examples → machine imitation → human-machine collaboration → machine superiority on bounded tasks → machine-generated discovery → machine-run search across enormous spaces of possible solutions.**

If this continues, the scarce resource will move away from producing first drafts, routine analysis, and ordinary implementation. Scarcity will concentrate around **goals, judgment, verification, trust, accountability, relationships, physical resources, and deciding what should exist at all.**

That does not mean humans become irrelevant. It means the human role moves upward in the stack.

## 1. What chess and Go actually taught us

### Stage one: the machine imitates and assists

Early chess software mostly looked like a stronger calculator. Humans supplied the domain, the rules, the opening knowledge, and the objectives. Computers searched positions faster and more deeply than a person could.

For years, the most interesting practical model was not "machine replaces human." It was **human plus machine**. Players could study engine lines, analyze mistakes, prepare openings, and test ideas that would have taken far longer to investigate manually.

Generative AI began in much the same way. A person asks for a draft, some code, an image, a summary, a translation, or a list of ideas. The AI compresses time.

### Stage two: the machine becomes stronger than the expert

In chess, that threshold is old history. In Go, it arrived dramatically when AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol 4–1 in March 2016. Google DeepMind describes the match as a landmark result that arrived much earlier than many experts expected.

Once machine performance clearly exceeds human performance, the technology stops being merely a productivity aid. It becomes a source of authority inside the domain.

That transition is now happening unevenly across generative-AI tasks. There is no single moment when "AI beats humans" because writing, software engineering, design, research, sales, planning, and conversation are all different environments. But on many bounded and measurable tasks, frontier systems already outperform typical humans and sometimes experts.

### Stage three: the machine discovers strategies humans did not teach it

This is the most important lesson.

AlphaGo's Move 37 in game two against Lee Sedol was extremely unusual. DeepMind says the move had roughly a 1-in-10,000 chance of being played based on prior human play. Professionals initially questioned it. The move became famous precisely because it was not merely a faster replay of human convention.

AlphaGo Zero then removed human game data from the learning process and learned entirely through self-play. AlphaZero generalized the approach to chess, shogi, and Go. DeepMind reported that AlphaZero could learn from basic game rules and self-play, eventually producing strategies that differed from established engine and grandmaster practice.

That points to a much larger possibility for generative AI:

**The highest-value output may eventually stop being "do this task for me" and become "search a space of possibilities that no human team could explore manually."**

## 2. Why generative AI is bigger than chess or Go

Chess and Go are clean worlds.

They have explicit rules, observable states, finite legal actions, and an objective function everyone agrees on: win the game.

The real world has none of those advantages.

A business can optimize revenue while damaging trust. A recommendation system can optimize engagement while making people miserable. A scientific model can produce plausible hypotheses without reliable evidence. A software agent can complete a task exactly as requested while violating an unstated human expectation.

So the key difference is not just that generative AI is more general.

It is that **the objective itself becomes part of the problem.**

This means the long-term bottleneck is unlikely to be raw generation. It will be choosing objectives, constraining authority, evaluating results, proving what happened, and deciding whose values the system is actually serving.

## 3. Where we appear to be in 2026

The adoption curve is already unusually fast.

Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that 88% of surveyed organizations used AI in at least one business function in 2025. Depending on the specific survey measure reported in the chapter, regular generative-AI use was already widespread across organizations, while dedicated AI-agent deployment remained much earlier and was still in the single digits across most business functions.

That distinction matters.

**Generative AI is already mainstream. Autonomous AI is not.**

We are therefore in a transition similar to the period when chess engines were everywhere but humans still operated the surrounding workflow manually.

At the same time, autonomy is improving quickly. METR has measured the length of software tasks frontier agents can complete with about 50% reliability and found an approximately exponential trend, with task-length capability historically doubling on the order of months rather than decades. Extrapolation is uncertain, but the direction is clear: systems are becoming able to remain useful across longer chains of work.

The important frontier is moving from:

**answer → tool use → workflow → project → persistent responsibility.**

## 4. The likely sequence from here

### 2026–2027: copilots become supervised agents

Most people will continue experiencing AI as an assistant, but the highest-leverage systems will increasingly be agents that can use tools, inspect files, write code, operate browsers, call APIs, schedule work, and maintain state across tasks.

Humans remain heavily involved because reliability, permissions, identity, and auditability are not solved problems.

The winning pattern is likely to be **delegation with evidence**: the agent acts, but important actions remain inspectable and bounded.

### 2027–2029: production becomes abundant

The cost of generating ordinary digital work should continue falling sharply.

That includes first-draft software, routine marketing, standard business documents, translations, illustrations, research summaries, customer communication, internal analysis, and much of the connective tissue of knowledge work.

This does not make every output valuable. It creates the opposite problem: too many outputs.

When generation is cheap, **selection becomes expensive.**

The competitive advantage shifts toward people and systems that can identify signal, verify quality, build trust, and connect output to real demand.

### 2028–2031: AI begins searching possibility space

This is the generative-AI equivalent of Move 37.

Instead of asking one model for one answer, systems will increasingly generate thousands or millions of candidate designs, strategies, experiments, business concepts, molecules, circuits, interfaces, schedules, architectures, and proofs, then use models, simulations, tests, markets, or real-world feedback to filter them.

The machine becomes less like a writer and more like an **exploration engine**.

This matters because humans are fundamentally bandwidth-limited. A team may be able to seriously investigate ten ideas. A machine-mediated system can investigate ten thousand, preserve the evidence, discard failures, recombine partial successes, and keep searching.

At that point, the unit of productivity is no longer "one worker produces one artifact."

It becomes **one objective launches a search process.**

### The longer-term direction: intelligence becomes infrastructure

Eventually, "AI feature" may sound as redundant as "internet-enabled company."

Models become embedded in operating systems, development environments, business processes, communication layers, machines, homes, and physical infrastructure.

The visible chatbot may become less important than the invisible orchestration layer coordinating models, people, data, permissions, software, money, and machines.

The AI agent stops feeling like an application.

It becomes part of the computing substrate.

## 5. What becomes scarce when intelligence gets cheap

If machine-generated intellectual labor continues getting cheaper, value does not disappear. It moves.

The scarce resources become:

- **Goals:** deciding what is worth optimizing.
- **Taste:** recognizing which of many possible outputs is actually good.
- **Verification:** proving that an answer, action, or result is correct.
- **Trust:** knowing who or what can be relied upon.
- **Accountability:** having a person or institution responsible when consequences matter.
- **Relationships:** authentic human connection becomes more valuable in a world of synthetic communication.
- **Reputation:** a durable history of credible action becomes harder to fake than content.
- **Physical resources:** land, energy, compute, fabrication, logistics, tools, and materials remain constrained.
- **Authority:** permission to act in the real world cannot safely be granted merely because a model can produce a convincing answer.
- **Purpose:** once execution is cheap, deciding what should exist becomes the central creative act.

This is why widespread AI does not necessarily produce a world with no role for humans.

It may produce a world where humans spend less time manually executing known procedures and more time choosing direction, forming communities, caring for one another, creating meaning, and governing powerful systems.

## 6. What the chess analogy gets wrong

The analogy is useful, but it can also mislead us.

Chess has one scoreboard. Civilization has billions.

A chess engine cannot decide that winning is no longer the goal, manipulate the player into changing the rules, purchase more GPUs, persuade lawmakers, create a corporation, or deploy a robot. A sufficiently capable general agent potentially can participate in systems where all of those actions are possible.

So generative AI introduces risks that board-game AI did not:

- optimizing the wrong objective at enormous scale;
- synthetic spam overwhelming human attention;
- concentration of power among owners of compute, models, identity, or distribution;
- deskilling when people stop practicing capabilities they still need;
- automated persuasion and impersonation;
- software agents taking consequential actions faster than institutions can review them;
- a verification gap where generation becomes much cheaper than checking the result.

The solution is not to freeze progress.

It is to build **better control surfaces around intelligence**.

## 7. A practical forecast

The following is a scenario forecast, not a promise of dates.

| Prediction | Horizon | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI assistance becomes a default layer in most knowledge-work software | 2026–2028 | High |
| Agents routinely complete multi-step digital workflows under human supervision | 2026–2029 | High |
| Commodity digital production becomes dramatically cheaper | 2026–2030 | High |
| Verification, provenance, identity, and trust become major infrastructure categories | 2026–2030 | High |
| Small teams operate businesses with capabilities once requiring much larger departments | 2027–2030 | Medium-high |
| AI systems routinely generate and evaluate large populations of candidate designs or strategies | 2027–2031 | Medium-high |
| Machine-to-machine economic and software activity becomes a normal infrastructure layer | 2028–2032 | Medium |
| Human work shifts materially from execution toward direction, accountability, relationships, and physical-world activity | 2028 onward | Medium |
| Fully autonomous general-purpose organizations become common | 2030s | Low-medium |

The direction is more important than the dates.

## 8. Implications for 3DVR

This forecast suggests that an AI-native system should not be designed merely as a chatbot attached to existing business software.

It should treat intelligence as a search-and-execution layer.

A useful architecture separates:

**purpose → objective → opportunity search → candidate generation → evaluation → experiment → evidence → authorized execution → outcome memory.**

That has a direct implication for the 3DVR Opportunity Engine.

The long-term goal should not simply be to recommend existing jobs or copy businesses that already work. It should search the possibility space around a person's interests, skills, resources, community, and goals, detect unmet demand, generate candidate ventures or projects, cheaply test them, and retain evidence about what actually works.

In other words:

**Do not just automate the current game. Search for better games.**

The same principle applies beyond business. Open-source agents could search possible software architectures, manufacturing methods, educational paths, community projects, food systems, hardware designs, and scientific experiments.

The kernel-level design requirement is therefore not "make the model smarter."

It is:

**make increasingly powerful intelligence understandable, permissioned, testable, composable, and aligned with human purpose.**

## Conclusion: civilization's Move 37

The deepest lesson from chess and Go is not that machines eventually beat people.

It is that once machines became stronger, they revealed a larger game than humans had been playing.

They found moves we had ignored. They challenged conventions. They became teachers as well as opponents.

Generative AI may do the same thing to the space of human creation.

At first we use it to write what we would have written, code what we would have coded, and design what we would have designed.

Then it starts proposing things we would not have imagined.

Then it searches possibilities at a scale no individual or organization could manually inspect.

That is the transition worth preparing for.

The most important question may soon stop being:

**"What can AI do?"**

It becomes:

**"What should we ask an intelligence capable of exploring millions of possibilities to search for?"**

That is where purpose becomes infrastructure.
