Natural materials.
For everyone.

Why should linen clothes and repairable shoes feel like luxury goods? Let's make the designs, costs, tradeoffs, and repair knowledge public.

A project hypothesis, not a claim that natural is always cheaper or better.

The problem

Cheap at checkout isn't always cheap over time.

Polyester

Highly scaled manufacturing often lowers the purchase price. It can be durable and quick-drying, but sheds synthetic microfibers and is fossil-derived.

Linen

Flax-based, breathable and often long-lasting. Processing, weaving, sewing, and smaller production runs can raise the upfront price.

Leather & replaceable soles

Some stitched or welted shoes can be resoled. Leather soles need care and may grip poorly on wet surfaces; rubber can be safer and more practical.

Measure, don't assume

Compare the full cost of ownership.

For each product, record purchase price, years of actual use, repairs, cleaning and maintenance, and end-of-life options. Divide total spending by years worn—or better, by wears—to compare alternatives fairly.

Example only: A $150 repairable shoe worn for 10 years with $80 in repairs costs $23 per year. A $50 pair replaced every two years costs $25 per year. Actual results depend on fit, use, labor, materials, and maintenance.

Natural fibers also have environmental costs: agriculture, water, dyeing, tanning, transport, and land use. Avoid blanket sustainability claims without lifecycle evidence.

The open experiment

Build the affordable alternative.

  1. Publish open patterns: garment dimensions, cutting layouts, materials, and repair methods.
  2. Prototype: start with the existing 3DVR linen drawstring pants; explore a replaceable-strap, replaceable-sole sandal next.
  3. Track real costs: materials, tools, maker labor, shipping, repairs, and cost per wear. Pay makers fairly.
  4. Test with people: comfort, durability, slip resistance, fit, wash cycles, and accessibility.
  5. Share improvements: patterns, failures, local suppliers, and alternatives for different budgets.

The principle

Open knowledge lowers barriers. Fair labor still matters.

Our goal isn't to pretend skilled labor is free. It's to reduce needless markup, waste, information gaps, and proprietary lock-in—while creating more ways to repair, reuse, and make locally. Offer a low-cost path for people who can't afford a premium upfront purchase.
