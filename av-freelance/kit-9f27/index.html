01 — Price the work

Set a rate you can defend.

Target day rate = (annual income goal + business overhead + tax/buffer) ÷ realistic billable days.

  • Choose one primary role you want to be called for: A1, A2, V1, V2, LED, projection, RF, comms, streaming, general AV lead, or another clear lane.
  • Write down three numbers: target rate, minimum acceptable rate, and walk-away rate.
  • Define what a day includes: expected hours, overtime, travel time, parking, per diem, gear, and cancellation terms.
  • Quote the job, not your old hourly wage. Freelance pricing has to cover unpaid admin, gaps between jobs, insurance, taxes, and equipment.

02 — Get called

Use short, human outreach.

Warm contact Hey [Name] — I’m opening up a little more freelance availability for [role]. If you ever need another solid [A1/A2/V1/etc.] in [city], I’d love to work together. My current day rate is [rate]. Happy to send availability anytime.

Production company Hi [Name/Team] — I’m a freelance [role] based in [city] with experience in [2–3 relevant areas]. I’m taking on more independent show work and would like to get into your technician pool. My current day rate is [rate]. Portfolio/resume: [link]. Thanks — [name]

Simple follow-up Hey [Name] — quick follow-up in case this got buried. I still have some availability for [month/date range] and would be glad to help with [role]. No pressure if you’re covered.

03 — Before saying yes

Confirm the job in writing.

  • Role: what exactly are you responsible for?
  • Date and call time: include expected end time and load-out.
  • Rate: day rate or hourly structure, minimum call, overtime trigger, travel, parking, per diem, and gear.
  • Location: venue, loading instructions, parking, credential requirements.
  • Client contact: name and mobile number for show day.
  • Payment: who receives the invoice, required PO/vendor paperwork, and payment terms.

04 — Run the show

Make yourself easy to rehire.

  • Arrive early enough to solve access, parking, credentials, and gear surprises before call time.
  • Confirm the chain of command and communication method.
  • Ask what “show ready” means for your system and when it must be ready.
  • Document important settings, patching, frequencies, IPs, file versions, or handoff notes as appropriate.
  • Before leaving, confirm you are released and ask where the invoice should go.
  • Send a short thank-you after the gig and keep the relationship alive without pestering people.

05 — Close the loop

Invoice fast. Track every job.

  • Invoice within 24 hours unless the client gives different instructions.
  • Include your name/business, invoice number, date, client, show/job, agreed rate, overtime or expenses, total, and payment instructions.
  • Track: booked date, client, role, rate, invoice sent date, due date, paid date, and whether you would work for them again.
  • Do not confuse booked revenue with cash received.

06 — 30-day transition

Build a runway, not a dramatic exit.

  1. Week 1

    Package yourself

    Choose your lane, rate, one short bio, and one link you can send.

  2. Week 2

    Reconnect

    Message people who already know your work. Aim for real relationships, not mass outreach.

  3. Week 3

    Take one independent job

    Run the full loop: confirm, work, invoice, follow up, record what happened.

  4. Week 4

    Measure repeatability

    Count repeat clients, booked days, average rate, and cash actually collected before changing your employment situation.

The rule

One good client is worth more than 100 cold leads.

Be reliable, easy to work with, technically sharp, and clear about money. The compounding asset is a small network of people who trust you enough to call again.
